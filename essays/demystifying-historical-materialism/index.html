Historical Materialism (HM) is a framework for understanding the dynamics of human societies, based on analyzing the specific material conditions and material interests that form the base of every human society. HM is a very intuitive way to look at history, to place history in its given society during a given period, and yet the predominant ideology has masked this simple intuitiveness.

But before we talk about class struggle, before we talk about means of production, before we talk about modes of production, capitalism, socialism, etc. etc. There is one key concept that builds the foundation of HM. It will appear again and again sometimes in direct terms, and sometimes implied. It's the concept of "necessity".

At its very core, HM is built upon the concept of necessity. In everyday language, necessity refers to something you cannot do without—a requirement, an obligation, or an essential condition for achieving a particular result. It’s the opposite of an option or a luxury.

And yet Friedrich Engels, one of the two formulators of HM, described freedom is the understanding of necessity. Let us look at the full quote, in order to understand this apparent contradiction:

SOURCE · ENGELS, ANTI-DÜHRING (1877)

"Hegel was the first to state correctly the relation between freedom and necessity. To him, freedom is the insight into necessity (die Einsicht in die Notwendigheit).

"Necessity is blind only in so far as it is not understood [begriffen]."

Freedom does not consist in any dreamt-of independence from natural laws, but in the knowledge of these laws, and in the possibility this gives of systematically making them work towards definite ends. This holds good in relation both to the laws of external nature and to those which govern the bodily and mental existence of men themselves — two classes of laws which we can separate from each other at most only in thought but not in reality. Freedom of the will therefore means nothing but the capacity to make decisions with knowledge of the subject. Therefore the freer a man’s judgment is in relation to a definite question, the greater is the necessity with which the content of this judgment will be determined; while the uncertainty, founded on ignorance, which seems to make an arbitrary choice among many different and conflicting possible decisions, shows precisely by this that it is not free, that it is controlled by the very object it should itself control. Freedom therefore consists in the control over ourselves and over external nature, a control founded on knowledge of natural necessity; it is therefore necessarily a product of historical development. The first men who separated themselves from the animal kingdom were in all essentials as unfree as the animals themselves, but each step forward in the field of culture was a step towards freedom. On the threshold of human history stands the discovery that mechanical motion can be transformed into heat: the production of fire by friction; at the close of the development so far gone through stands the discovery that heat can be transformed into mechanical motion: the steam-engine. — And, in spite of the gigantic liberating revolution in the social world which the steam-engine is carrying through, and which is not yet half completed, it is beyond all doubt that the generation of fire by friction has had an even greater effect on the liberation of mankind. For the generation of fire by friction gave man for the first time control over one of the forces of nature, and thereby separated him for ever from the animal kingdom. The steam-engine will never bring about such a mighty leap forward in human development, however important it may seem in our eyes as representing all those immense productive forces dependent on it — forces which alone make possible a state of society in which there are no longer class distinctions or anxiety over the means of subsistence for the individual, and in which for the first time there can be talk of real human freedom, of an existence in harmony with the laws of nature that have become known. But how young the whole of human history still is, and how ridiculous it would be to attempt to ascribe any absolute validity to our present views, is evident from the simple fact that all past history can be characterised as the history of the epoch from the practical discovery of the transformation of mechanical motion into heat up to that of the transformation of heat into mechanical motion.

- Anti-Dühring by Frederick Engels (1877) chapter XI. Freedom and Necessity"

Mao Zedong, a prominent Marxist figure, also added on:

SOURCE · MAO ZEDONG (1941)

Knowledge of the world is for the purpose of transforming the world; the history of humankind is created by humankind itself. However, if one has no knowledge of the world then the world cannot be transformed; "without revolutionary theory there can be no revolutionary movement." Our high-and-mighty dogmatists are ignorant of this point. It is through the two processes of knowledge and transformation that the realm of necessity will be changed into the realm of freedom. The European philosophers of the past comprehended the truth that "freedom is knowledge of necessity." Marx's contribution lay not in the rejection of this truth, but in recognizing that this truth was incomplete, and his addition of the truth that "the transformation of the world" is founded on knowledge of necessity. "Freedom is knowledge of necessity"-this is the proposition of the philosophers of the past. "Freedom is knowledge of necessity and the transformation of the world"-this is Marx's proposition. It is a poor Marxist who does not understand that knowledge of the world is premised on the transformation of the world, and that the transformation of the world is premised on knowledge of the world. Any Chinese Marxist who does not understand that knowledge of China is premised on the desire to transform China, and that the transformation of China is premised on knowledge of China, is indeed a poor Chinese Marxist. Marx said that man differs from the honeybee in that man has in his mind a design for his house prior to constructing it. The house that we will construct is the house of the Chinese Revolution, and we too must firstly have a design for the Chinese Revolution. Not only must we have a grand design, a general plan, we must also have many designs which are subdivisions of the grand design, which are designs on a smaller scale. And these designs can be nothing but the active reflection attained in the practice of the Chinese Revolution of objective, real situations. The reason why our high-and-mighty dogmatists are subjectivists is because their entire design for the revolution, whether on a large or small scale, general or subdivided, is not based on and nor does it conform to objective reality. They have only the subjective aspiration to transform the world, or China, or Northern China, or the cities; they have no presentable plan. Their plan is not scientific, but is subjective and arbitrary, is a complete muddle. Since they have no knowledge whatever of the world and wish to transform it in a rash way, the consequence will not only be that they suffer cracked skulls themselves, but that the masses will also meet the same fate under their leadership. Since these high-and-mighty dogmatists are blind when it comes to the realm of necessity of the Chinese revolution, their presumptuous desire to play the part of the leaders of the people would truly be a case of "a blind man riding a blind horse at the edge of a deep gorge at midnight."

- Freedom is Knowledge of Necessity and the Transformation of the World by Mao Zedong (1941)

It is very clear that the concept of necessity influenced heavily on their thinking, as they used HM to analyze and to guide their actions in changing the world.

SHEET 03NECESSITY & SUFFICIENCY, FORMAL

We can try to frame their thinking in a more modern language. Let's look into the concepts of "necessity and sufficiency" in formal logic:

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA, “NECESSITY AND SUFFICIENCY” (2026)

In logic and mathematics, necessity and sufficiency are terms used to describe a conditional or implicational relationship between two statements. For example, in the conditional statement: "If P then Q", Q is necessary for P, because the truth of Q is "necessarily" guaranteed by the truth of P. (Equivalently, it is impossible to have P without Q, or the falsity of Q ensures the falsity of P.) Similarly, P is sufficient for Q, because P being true always or "sufficiently" implies that Q is true, but P not being true does not always imply that Q is not true.

In general, a necessary condition is one (possibly one of several conditions) that must be present in order for another condition to occur, while a sufficient condition is one that produces the said condition.[3] The assertion that a statement is a "necessary and sufficient" condition of another means that the former statement is true if and only if the latter is true. That is, the two statements must be either simultaneously true, or simultaneously false.

In ordinary English (also natural language) "necessary" and "sufficient" often indicate relations between conditions or states of affairs, not statements. For example, being round is a necessary condition for being a circle, but is not sufficient since ovals and ellipses are round but not circles – while being a circle is a sufficient condition for being round. Any conditional statement consists of at least one sufficient condition and at least one necessary condition.

- Wikipedia article "Necessity and sufficiency" (retrieved in June 2026)

NOTES

For example:

1. For the whole numbers greater than two, being odd is necessary to being prime, since even numbers greater than two are always divisible by two, the only even number that is prime.

2. The sun being above the horizon is a necessary condition for direct sunlight; but it is not a sufficient condition, as something else may be casting a shadow, e.g., the moon in the case of an eclipse.

3. Consider thunder, the sound caused by lightning. One says that thunder is necessary for lightning, since lightning never occurs without thunder. Whenever there is lightning, there is thunder. The thunder does not cause the lightning (since lightning causes thunder), but because lightning always comes with thunder, we say that thunder is necessary for lightning. (That is, in its formal sense, necessity doesn't imply causality.)

4. Being at least 30 years old is necessary for serving in the U.S. Senate. If you are under 30 years old, then it is impossible for you to be a senator. That is, if you are a senator, it follows that you must be at least 30 years old.

5. For it to be true that "John is a bachelor", it is necessary that it be also true that John is

- unmarried,

- male,

- adult,

since to state "John is a bachelor" implies he has each of those three additional predicates.

6. "John is a king" implies that John is male. So, knowing that John is a king is sufficient to knowing that he is a male.

7. A number's being divisible by four is sufficient (but not necessary) for it to be even, but being divisible by two is both sufficient and necessary for it to be even.

8. An occurrence of thunder is a sufficient condition for the occurrence of lightning in the sense that hearing thunder, and unambiguously recognizing it as such, justifies concluding that there has been a lightning bolt.

9. That the center of a playing card should be marked with a single large spade (♠) is sufficient for the card to be an ace. Three other sufficient conditions are that the center of the card be marked with a single diamond (♦), heart (♥), or club (♣). None of these conditions is necessary to the card's being an ace, but their disjunction is, since no card can be an ace without fulfilling at least (in fact, exactly) one of these conditions.

With this, we can try to grasp what Engels or Mao intended to convey in more modern language.

Take Engels first. Let K stand for knowledge of natural and social laws (understanding necessity). Let F stand for freedom (control over ourselves and external nature). Engels argues that K is necessary for F. You cannot have freedom without understanding the laws that govern reality. Then Engels claimed "and in which for the first time there can be talk of real human freedom, of an existence in harmony with the laws of nature that have become known" which means to Engels, F is a sufficient condition for N. Engels considered the development of steam engine was built upon the development of knowledge of physics, of heat into mechanical motion. To Engels, this knowledge of necessity was one of the necessary conditions that made possible for there to be real serious consideration of real human freedom for everyone.

Mao expanded on Engels's argument further. Let K stand for knowledge of the world (the realm of necessity). Let T stand for the transformation of the world (the realm of freedom). Mao agrees that K is necessary for T. Without revolutionary theory, without knowledge of objective reality, you cannot transform the world in a revolutionary manner. For Mao, which he cited as Marx's argument, T equals freedom, because K alone is not sufficient for freedom. Revolutionary theory is useless without practice. However Mao's specific argument above was directed at dogmatists, who ignored the necessity of K, believing only practice as necessary for T. To Mao, the transformation of the world in a revolutionary manner, the realm of freedom, is "The house that we will construct is the house of the Chinese Revolution, and we too must firstly have a design for the Chinese Revolution." Mao is arguing that the Chinese Revolution (T) is a sufficient condition for K "Not only must we have a grand design, a general plan, we must also have many designs which are subdivisions of the grand design, which are designs on a smaller scale. And these designs can be nothing but the active reflection attained in the practice of the Chinese Revolution of objective, real situations." and revolutionary practice (the construction of the house).

Guided by the concept of necessity, we can elucidate their definition of freedom. Freedom, therefore, is not the absence of boundaries. Freedom is knowing exactly where the boundaries are, understanding how they work, and leveraging that knowledge to consciously build a better world.

SHEET 04MEANS OF SUBSISTENCE

So, now we can go deeper into the logic of HM. What are these boundaries exactly? How does HM help us understand these boundaries? Let's look into another quote from Engels:

SOURCE · ENGELS AT MARX’S GRAVE (1883)

Just as Darwin discovered the law of development of organic nature, so Marx discovered the law of development of human history: the simple fact, hitherto concealed by an overgrowth of ideology, that mankind must first of all eat, drink, have shelter and clothing, before it can pursue politics, science, art, religion, etc.; that therefore the production of the immediate material means, and consequently the degree of economic development attained by a given people or during a given epoch, form the foundation upon which the state institutions, the legal conceptions, art, and even the ideas on religion, of the people concerned have been evolved, and in the light of which they must, therefore, be explained, instead of vice versa, as had hitherto been the case.

- Frederick Engels’ Speech at the Grave of Karl Marx (March 17, 1883)

This is a relatively clear and straightforward quote, but let's break this down further. We have hit the first derivation of "necessity" in the abstract concept into a more concrete concept. The concept of "material needs" which is something you need to survive (food, water, clothing, housing, etc.). Other Marxists have many different names for this which include but not limited to: means of subsistence, means of sustenance, necessaries, necessities, basic goods, etc. etc. We will call it MoS (short for either means of subsistence or means of sustenance) for simplicity.

Let "politics, science, art, religion, etc." be called higher pursuits. The core argument of HM is that fulfillment of MoS is necessary for higher pursuits, and that conversely, higher pursuits are sufficient for the fulfillment of MoS. Engels claimed if you want to analyze why this-or-that society in a given time period has this-or-that politics, religion, philosophy, ideas, and/or even culture then you must first investigate the material conditions and the material interests that are at play of said society within said time period. And this material analysis must be done before other kinds of analysis such as psychological factors. But what is "material interest"? Isn't "interest" in of itself subjective? Don't people have different needs, preference, etc.? What is the usefulness of "material analysis"?

NOTES

If people are unconvinced by the core argument of HM, then allow me to state relatively uncontroversial facts:

1. Humans are material beings that have material needs like food, water, shelter, etc. in order to continue living. Without these, humans will die.

2. Dead people can't do things. In the sense they aren't agents that actively "pursue politics, science, art, religion, etc."

3. Humans are an altricial species. Meaning, human infants are born helpless and require the care of other grown humans lest human infants will die. In another word, infants aren't independent agents.

4. All humans were once infants.

5. Society is composed of humans.

By the time a human has grown and can already do things such as having higher pursuit, or *gasp* having suicidal thoughts (what an absurd argument, Camus!), they have by necessity consumed MoS to reach that point in life. And their MoS was provided by other humans who themselves needed to fulfill their own MoS and were once also infants. And because no humans mean no society, this necessity applies to any and all kinds of societies as well.

Some people may push back and argue that humans are born and raised by the conscious choice of their parent(s), that we therefore actually need to consider beyond pure "necessity" and into the realm of psychology or subjectivity or otherwise, in order to understand choices and rationality of humans. To that I say: okay. Let's take that logic to the extreme. Your parents also have parents, and they too have their own parents. What happens if we keep following this chain to their parents and then their parents? Follow the chain all the way back to pre-Homo sapiens direct ancestors. What about when our ancestors were still single-celled? Can we still apply the logic of choices and human rationality? They too also have their own material needs. Of course, though they might not be altricial or fulfill their material needs anywhere close to what human does… but the primary concept, the key important concept of our analysis here is necessity. Even if you argue that humans are now different than our ancestors and thus the logic of human rationality should still apply, my point is this: humans may have various different reasons to make choices of making new humans or let them born, or to raise them. But if we collapse down the "why" human does this and instead ask yes-no questions: Do humans still make children? Do humans still raise children?

The answer to both is yes. It is just a matter-of-fact, and the "why" here is not relevant to our subject of study.

However, HM is NOT deterministic. Consider one of our examples earlier from logical necessity and sufficiency:

Consider thunder, the sound caused by lightning. One says that thunder is necessary for lightning, since lightning never occurs without thunder. Whenever there is lightning, there is thunder. The thunder does not cause the lightning (since lightning causes thunder), but because lightning always comes with thunder, we say that thunder is necessary for lightning. (That is, in its formal sense, necessity doesn't imply causality.)

HM is NOT saying that fulfillment of MoS causes humans to have higher pursuit or determine humans what kind of higher pursuits they will have. It is a common strawman to consider that HM is saying "politics, science, art, and/or religion are created to justify and help humans acquiring their MoS." It is a historical outlook of viewing how the necessity of acquiring MoS is fulfilled under different societies. What material analysis is, is to ask how people go about their daily lives in order to acquire their material needs. Different geography, different time period, different cultures, and/or different social organization can all contribute to differing ways people can fulfill their MoS. Meaning there are many different kinds of sufficient conditions for humans to fulfill their MoS.

SHEET 05LABOUR

When we ask further "is there something necessary in order to acquire MoS?" we find that it always involve expending human labor. Even if an apple grew by itself completely naturally, someone still needs to make the effort of picking them and possibly transporting them to those who would consume it. We need human labor in order to produce our MoS, but human labor is only possible for humans who are independent agents, i.e., not infants. It's a chain that we can abstract to claim that infants need the past labor of humans that were born before them in order to provide their MoS before they themselves can perform labor.

There are many concrete types of labor such as farming the land, digging for a well, repairing tools, etc. But we can abstract them as simply "labor" and we understand that labor takes time and energy. HM primarily cares more about time because it's much easier to compare by a common standard. Here, we can introduce the term "labor-power" (LP), which is sometimes called "manpower" or "human resource". LP is your ability and capacity to do labor. It is tied to your physical body itself. You need a healthy mind and body to maintain your LP, which means consuming MoS healthily.

NOTES

To summarize the facts:

1. You need to do labor in order to acquire MoS.

2. You need LP to do labor.

3. You need to consume MoS to maintain your LP.

We don't have to go endlessly in circles. We can just abstract this as if analyzing the immediate humans that were here before your birth, who made it possible in acquiring the MoS for present and future humans who would develop their own LP. We can then simply ask what social systems are in play? How does society keep reproducing itself?

SHEET 06CLASS

The security of fulfilling a stable and predictable material needs for themselves and their family is the "material interest". You have certain forms of labor to fulfill your MoS, or a certain way of life in order to fulfill your MoS. This material interest is beyond mere psychology that if you deviate from your material interest, you objectively change your access to MoS which can impact your health or survival.

A group of people who have converging material interest where they can potentially co-operate or compete forms a social class or commonly called a class. Different classes that have diverging interests may also co-operate or compete. For classes that have completely opposing interest, i.e., where their material interests exist as zero-sum, they will struggle against each other or make compromises, this is a class struggle. A society with different classes, especially classes with opposing interests, is called a classed society.

Internal competition amongst the same class is not antagonistic, that they can potentially cooperate as they can compete as well which doesn't necessarily mean the loss for the other individual. Different classes may have different interests but that are ultimately not zero-sum, so their relationship is not antagonistic as well. They can cooperate or compete which don't necessarily mean the loss for the other class. When HM talks of "class struggle" it is always in the sense of classes that have diametrically opposing material interests, that a gain in one is a loss for the other. HM describes them as "antagonistic" and that they will struggle against one another or compromise, i.e., to settle for some material gain and loss mutually without the total defeat of the one class or total gain of the other.

Another important distinction is that infant or elderly people are not a distinct class, because two infants being provided MoS by parents of differing classes would have different material interests. The class structure is baked into the objective social structure of classed society as an organization that reproduces itself.

SHEET 07SURPLUS

And now, we need to understand perhaps the second most important concept in HM. The concept of "surplus". At its core, surplus means an amount that is more than what is needed or used. It is the excess that remains after all requirements or needs have been met.

A society needs to produce certain amount of MoS to maintain the same amount of humans, who are in turn needed to produce those MoS. It's a "necessary product" if humans need to produce this much in order for the population or quality of life not to fall. It's a "surplus product" when humans can produce more than it needs to maintain reproducing the society as an organism at the same level.

"Necessary labor time" is the time needed for you to keep doing labor in order to produce the necessary products, as in, for you to produce products equivalent to what you would need to consume in order to maintain your LP. "Surplus labor" is the labor that you do after you labor as long as the necessary labor time. The products you make in your surplus labor is the surplus product.

In case it's not clear already, humans can produce more than what humans need to consume in order to maintain their LP. Which means when you are working for a certain period of time, there's a necessary labor time that you actually need to do, in order to recuperate society providing your MoS. If you work beyond the necessary labor time, you would produce surplus which society can use to grow larger than it already is.

A classed society is only possible in a society that produces surplus. In other words, it's a society that produce more than enough that some humans don't need to produce MoS, that they can do other things in order to acquire MoS.

SHEET 08CAPITAL — SHEET PENDING

Let's analyze how this works under capitalism, because most modern societies in this world are capitalist societies. And please keep in mind, that this is an analysis of what generally happens. This is NOT about what "should be" or what is "good" or "better". Any disagreements in this analysis should pertain to the facts and not of opinion. If an ecologist were to make the claim "most fish of this species are eaten by predators before reaching maturity," and someone replied "well, a fish could just swim faster!" Then most people would understand this is a category error. Since I will be making claims about humans under capitalism in a similar manner, I ask people to please refrain from replying with a "solution" of what an individual human could do. But I welcome correction or challenging the facts.
