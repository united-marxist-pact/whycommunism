spoken · the reader's introductionWelcome to the fifth video in the series, The Character of the Dictatorship of the Proletariat According to Marx and Engels. Today we’ll be going over the 1852 piece, Marx’s Letter to Weydemeyer, on March 5th, which I subtitled “Proletarian rule normalizes social relations through force and organic development.” And to those who may or may not remember, in the preface, I utilize a quotation from this. So —

It is important that the Proletariat be familiar with historical economic theories in the sense that knowledge of history will allow them to understand the development and shifts of society and know, despite what intellectual labor towards analyzing the mode of production at any time always results in (that is, the belief that the current ruling order is a natural, normal and fair relationship that is harmonious and in principle equal and fair, rather than conditions of struggle and antagonism). Very often, people are embedded in conceptions of the "now" and never think of the future except in comparison. From the perspective of those people, the new relations in the Dictatorship will seem unfair, unharmonious, unnatural, and unequal, but those in the new society will understand it as the opposite.

The Dictatorship's social relations will become normalized and recognized as fair and just, establishing itself through force in the form of proletarian democracy, just as previous ruling classes have done with their own organically developing state and dictatorship. Class struggle is historically contingent on historical phases in the development of production, resulting in a new ruling class establishing itself through force - first through violent revolution, and then through the transformed state, which utilizes democracy as a tool of repression of the bourgeois minority. This is a class dictatorship, one that the Proletariat will establish for itself, as all historic ruling classes have. However, because the Proletariat is the last class, its victory will lead to its own abolition; the Dictatorship is nothing more than a transition to the abolition of all classes and to a classless society.

QUOTATION EXTRACTS

How, in the United States bourgeois society is still far too immature for the class struggle to be made perceptible and comprehensible; striking proof of this is provided by C. H. Carey (of Philadelphia),1 " the only North American economist of any note. He attacks Ricardo, the most classic representativec of the bourgeoisie and the most stoical opponent of the proletariat, as a man whose works are an arsenal for anarchists and socialists, for all enemies of the bourgeois order.d He accuses not only him, but also Malthus, Mill, Say, Torrens, Wakefield, MacCulloch, Senior, Whately, R. Jones, etc.—those who lead the economic dance in Europe—of tearing society apart, and of paving the way for civil war by showing that the economic bases of the various classes are such that they will inevitably give rise to a necessary and ever-growing antagonism between the latter. He tries to refute them, not, it is true, like the fatuous Heinzen, by relating the existence of classes to the existence of political privileges and monopolies, but by seeking to demonstrate that economic conditions: rent (landed property), profit (capital) and wages (wage labour),' rather than being conditions of struggle and antagonism, are conditions of association and harmony. All he proves, of course, is that the 'undeveloped' relations in the United States are, to him, 'normal relations'.

  1. Under any mode of production, what is considered "fair" and "just" social relations is identical to that mode of production. Hence, while the social relations in Socialism and the Dictatorship may seem unfair currently, they will cease being understood as such by the new society.
  2. The Dictatorship's social relations will become normalized and recognized as fair and just

Now as for myself, I do not claim to have discovered either the existence of classes in modern society or the struggle between them. Long before me, bourgeois historians had described the historical development of this struggle between the classes, as had bourgeois economists their economic anatomy. My own contribution was

1. to show that the existence of classes is merely bound up with certain historical phases in the development of production;

2. that the class struggle necessarily leads to the dictatorship of the proletariat;

3. that this dictatorship itself constitutes no more than a transition to the abolition of all classes and to a classless society.

  1. Class struggle is historically contingent on historical phases in the development of production, and this class struggle will result in a ruling class establishing itself through force as the ruling class. This is the class dictatorship, one of which the Proletariat will establish for itself.
  2. However, because the Proletariat is the last class, its victory will lead to its own abolition; the Dictatorship is nothing more than a transition to the abolition of all classes and to a classless society
